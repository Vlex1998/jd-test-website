A roofline should be a straight line. When you stand across the street and see a dip, a wave, or a bowed ridge, something underneath has given way or was never adequate. Some causes are cosmetic and cheap to fix. Others are structural and urgent. Here is how to tell which you are looking at.

Where the Sag Is Tells You a Lot

  • A dip between rafters (a wavy look across the slope, with low spots a couple feet wide) is usually decking: plywood or OSB that has gotten wet, delaminated, or was too thin for the rafter spacing.
  • A sag in the middle of a slope that runs along the rafter direction suggests rafters that are undersized, cracked, or have been cut.
  • A sagging ridge (the peak of the roof bows downward in the middle) often means the rafters are spreading at the walls, ceiling joists or collar ties are missing or cut, or the ridge board is failing.
  • A sag at one corner or along one wall can be foundation movement or a failing wall top plate, not a roof issue at all.

Common Causes in Houston Homes

Wet decking. The most common. A long-term leak at a chimney, valley, or pipe boot soaks the decking, it loses strength, and it sags between rafters. Once the leak is fixed and the roof replaced, that decking is replaced sheet by sheet. See how to find a roof leak.

Thin decking. Some tract homes from the 1970s through 1990s used 3/8-inch plywood or thin OSB on 24-inch rafter spacing. It sags between rafters under its own weight and the shingles, especially after years of attic heat. It may be sound enough to roof over, or it may need to be replaced.

Multiple shingle layers. A second layer roughly doubles the dead load. On marginal framing, that is enough to cause sag. See can you put new shingles over old ones.

Cut or missing framing. HVAC installers, electricians, and previous owners sometimes cut rafters, collar ties, or ceiling joists to fit equipment or make attic room. Each cut weakens the structure.

Undersized framing. Older homes, additions, and some converted porches and garages were framed lighter than code would require now.

Termite or rot damage to rafters or the top plate.

Foundation movement. Houston's clay soil moves. When a slab heaves or settles, the walls move with it and the roofline follows. If you also see cracks in drywall, doors that stick, or gaps at the baseboards, start with a foundation inspection, not a roofer.

Storm impact. A tree limb or a derecho gust can crack rafters or push the roof out of square.

How Urgent Is It?

  • Slight waviness between rafters with no leak: cosmetic, address at the next roof replacement.
  • A soft spot you can feel underfoot: wet decking, fix soon before it becomes a hole.
  • A visible ridge sag or a sag that is getting worse: get a structural evaluation now. Spreading rafters can push walls out.
  • Sag plus cracking sounds, new drywall cracks, or doors binding: call someone today. Do not wait for the next storm.

How It Is Fixed

  • Decking: replaced during a re-roof. The roofer identifies bad sheets after tear-off, swaps them, and charges per sheet. See hidden costs of roof replacement.
  • Rafters: sistered (a new rafter bolted alongside the damaged one), or replaced. Done from the attic in most cases.
  • Ridge sag: collar ties or rafter ties added, sometimes a structural ridge beam with posts. This needs an engineer's design in many cases.
  • Foundation: piers and leveling by a foundation company, after which the roof may partly straighten on its own.

Roofers handle decking and can usually handle rafter sistering. Ridge and wall problems need a framing contractor and often a structural engineer. A good roofer will tell you which one you need rather than guessing.

Insurance

Sagging from long-term leaks, rot, or inadequate construction is excluded as wear, neglect, or defective construction. Sagging caused by a specific storm event (limb impact, wind) can be covered. See does insurance cover roof leaks.
