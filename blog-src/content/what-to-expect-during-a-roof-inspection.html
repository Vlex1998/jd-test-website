"Free roof inspection" means very different things depending on who is offering it. Sometimes it is a 45-minute walk of the roof and attic with photos and a written report. Sometimes it is a salesperson looking at the roof from the driveway and telling you it needs to be replaced. Here is what a real inspection includes, how long it takes, and how to tell which kind you are getting.

How Long It Takes

A thorough residential inspection takes 30 to 60 minutes on a typical home, longer on a large or complex roof. Anyone who is done in ten minutes did not inspect the roof.

What Gets Inspected on the Roof

  • Shingles. Granule loss, cracking, curling, blistering, missing or creased tabs, and overall remaining life. On a hail inspection, chalked test squares and hit counts. See how adjusters count hail hits.
  • Flashing. Chimneys, walls, valleys, and the drip edge. Rust, gaps, failed sealant, and whether the flashing was done right in the first place.
  • Penetrations. Every pipe boot, vent, exhaust cap, skylight, and satellite mount.
  • Ridge and hips. Cap condition, ridge vent attachment, and sealing.
  • Soft metals. Gutters, vents, and AC fins, which show hail impacts clearly.
  • Decking. Soft or spongy areas felt underfoot, which mean rot.
  • Gutters and drainage. Debris, pitch, attachment, and granule buildup, which is a shingle-age indicator. See gutters and roof drainage.
  • Moss, algae, and debris.

What Gets Inspected in the Attic

Many inspectors skip this. They should not. The attic shows:

  • Water stains and trails on the underside of the decking and on rafters
  • Daylight around penetrations
  • Wet or compressed insulation
  • Ventilation adequacy and blocked soffit vents. See roof ventilation and attic heat.
  • Mold or condensation signs
  • Decking type and condition

What You Should Get Afterward

  • Photos of every problem found, with enough context to tell where on the roof it is.
  • A written summary: the roof's approximate age and condition, specific problems, and a recommendation (no action, repair, or replace) with reasons.
  • A priced estimate for any recommended work, itemized. See how to read a roofing estimate.
  • An honest answer about whether storm damage exists and whether it is worth an insurance claim.

Inspection vs Sales Pitch

Warning signs you are getting a pitch rather than an inspection:

  • No one gets on the roof or in the attic
  • No photos, or photos that could be from any roof
  • The answer is "full replacement" before anyone has looked closely
  • Pressure to sign the same day, or to file an insurance claim on the spot
  • Someone who knocked on your door unannounced after a storm. See local roofer vs storm chaser.

A good inspector will tell you when the roof is fine. We do that regularly. A roof with five years left and no active leaks does not need to be replaced today, and saying so builds the kind of trust that brings people back when it does.

Drone Inspections

Drones are useful for steep or fragile roofs and for overview photos. They are not a substitute for touching the roof. A drone cannot feel a soft spot in the decking, press on a hail bruise, or lift a shingle to check the seal. A combination of a walk and drone photos is the best of both.

How Often to Inspect

Once a year is reasonable for a roof over ten years old, plus after any significant storm. Many homeowners do spring (before hurricane season) and after the first hail event of the year. See our seasonal roof maintenance checklist.
