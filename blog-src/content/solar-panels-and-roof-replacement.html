Solar panels are a 25-year commitment bolted to a roof that may not have 25 years left. If the roof fails under the panels, every panel has to come off, the roof gets replaced, and the panels go back on, at a cost of several thousand dollars on top of the roof. The order of operations matters. Here is how to think about it and what to coordinate.

The Basic Rule

If your roof has less than 10 to 15 years of realistic life left, replace it before the panels go on. Houston asphalt shingles rarely last past 20 years, so a roof over 8 to 10 years old is usually a candidate. See how long a roof lasts in Texas. A roofer's inspection will tell you where you stand. See what to expect during a roof inspection.

Why Panel Removal and Reinstall Is Expensive

Solar installers charge for detach and reset, and the price is not trivial. The system has to be shut down and disconnected, racking unbolted, panels staged, and everything reinstalled and recommissioned, often with a new permit and utility inspection. Add roofer scheduling around the solar crew, and a roof replacement under existing panels can cost far more than it would have before the panels were installed. Some solar warranties also have conditions about who removes the panels.

What to Specify on a Roof That Will Get Solar

  • Longer-life shingles. Since the roof needs to outlast the panels, consider a premium or Class 4 architectural shingle rather than the base product. See Class 4 impact-resistant shingles.
  • Full synthetic underlayment and ice and water shield in the area where the array will go. Every racking foot is a penetration, and the underlayment is the backup.
  • Decking check. Racking is lagged into rafters through the decking. Any soft decking in the array area must be replaced.
  • Fewer penetrations in the array zone. Plumbing vents and exhaust caps can sometimes be relocated during the re-roof to keep the array area clear.
  • New pipe boots and vents everywhere, so nothing under or next to the array needs service for a long time. See pipe boot leaks.
  • Ridge vent and good attic ventilation. Panels shade the roof and run cooler, but the attic still needs airflow. See roof ventilation and attic heat.

Metal and Solar

Standing seam metal is a strong pairing with solar. Panels clamp to the seams with no roof penetrations at all, and the roof will outlast the panels. If you are replacing the roof anyway and considering solar, price out standing seam. See metal roofing in Texas.

Coordinating Roofer and Solar Installer

  • Have the solar company's layout before the roof goes on, so the roofer can plan penetrations and the solar crew can confirm rafter locations.
  • Make sure the roof warranty covers solar mounting. Some manufacturer warranties require the solar installer to use specific flashing on the mounts, and some are voided by careless installs. Get it in writing from both parties.
  • Ask the solar installer who is responsible if a mount leaks. It should be them, with a written workmanship warranty on their penetrations.
  • Sequence: roof first, then a few weeks for the shingles to seal in the heat, then solar.

Hail, Storms, and Insurance

Panels take hail better than most people expect; tempered glass is rated for large hailstones. The roof around and under the panels still gets inspected after a storm, and panels complicate both the inspection and the repair. Tell your insurer when solar is installed; many carriers cover the system under the dwelling policy but need it documented. If the roof is damaged in a storm, the claim should include detach and reset of the array. See Texas roof insurance claim guide.

Already Have Panels on an Old Roof?

Get the roof inspected now rather than after it leaks. If replacement is coming, plan it in cooler months when the solar crew and roofer can coordinate, and get detach and reset quotes from the solar company and, if your installer is gone, from independent solar service companies. Some roofing companies work with solar partners to handle both sides.

Tax Credits

Federal solar credits have historically included some roof work directly needed to support the array, but the rules have changed recently. Do not assume roof costs qualify. See is a new roof tax deductible and ask a CPA.
