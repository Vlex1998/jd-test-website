A denied roof claim is frustrating, but it is not the end of the process. Insurers deny or underpay claims for a handful of predictable reasons, and many denials can be reversed with better documentation or a second inspection. Here is how to read the denial, figure out which category you are in, and what to do about it.

Common Reasons Roof Claims Are Denied in Texas

  • Wear and tear. The adjuster concluded the damage is from age, not a storm. This is the most common reason and the most arguable.
  • Not enough damage. The adjuster found some hail or wind damage but below the threshold the carrier uses for replacement. See how many hail hits it takes.
  • Late reporting. Texas allows carriers to require prompt notice, and most policies say damage must be reported within a reasonable time, often interpreted as one year or less. Filing for a storm from three years ago is an uphill climb.
  • Pre-existing or unrepaired damage. Damage from a previous storm that was paid but never fixed.
  • Excluded cause. Flooding, earth movement, neglect, and faulty installation are typically excluded.
  • Below deductible. The damage is real, but the repair cost is less than your wind and hail deductible, so there is nothing to pay.

Step 1: Get the Denial in Writing

Texas law requires your insurer to state the reason for a denial in writing. If you only got a phone call, ask for the letter and the adjuster's report, including photos and the estimate. You cannot argue with a decision you cannot read.

Step 2: Have Your Own Roofer Inspect

If you have not already, get a thorough inspection from a local roofing company. Not a drive-by. Someone who gets on the roof, photographs every slope, marks hail hits with chalk, checks soft metals like vents and gutters for impact marks, and writes it up. This report, with dated photos, is your evidence. Our hail damage guide and wind damage signs explain what a good inspection looks for.

Step 3: Confirm the Storm

Weather records matter. The National Weather Service storm reports, hail-tracking services, and local news coverage can show that hail of a certain size or wind of a certain speed hit your neighborhood on a specific date. If your claim was denied as wear and tear but there is a documented two-inch hail event over your house, that is a strong argument.

Step 4: Request a Re-Inspection

Call your claims representative, say that you disagree with the findings, and ask for a re-inspection with a different adjuster. Have your roofer attend. Most carriers will grant one re-inspection without much pushback, and a second set of eyes with your roofer pointing out damage often changes the result.

Step 5: Invoke Appraisal if Your Policy Has It

Most Texas homeowners policies include an appraisal clause. It is meant for disputes over the amount of a loss rather than whether a loss is covered, but it is a powerful tool when the carrier agrees there is some damage and the argument is about how much. Each side hires an appraiser, the two pick an umpire, and the decision is binding on the amount. You pay your appraiser and half the umpire. It is worth it on a full roof dispute, not on a small repair.

Step 6: Complain to TDI

The Texas Department of Insurance accepts consumer complaints against insurers and will contact the carrier on your behalf. It does not decide claims, but a TDI complaint gets attention inside the company and creates a record. It is free and can be filed online.

Step 7: Consider a Public Adjuster or Attorney

For large, clear-cut losses where the carrier will not move, a licensed public adjuster (who works for a percentage of the recovery) or an attorney who handles insurance claims can help. Be realistic: on a modest roof claim, their fee may eat most of the gain. Texas requires public adjusters to be licensed through TDI.

What Not to Do

  • Do not let a roofer "handle the claim" for you in exchange for signing a contract. Texas law restricts roofers from acting as adjusters, and deductible waivers are illegal.
  • Do not make permanent repairs before the dispute is resolved, unless water is coming in. Temporary protection is fine and expected. See emergency roof tarping.
  • Do not file repeatedly for the same damage hoping for a different answer. It damages your claim history.

When the Denial Is Right

Sometimes the roof really is just old. A 20-year-old roof with a few hail marks is not a claim, it is a replacement you will pay for. That is disappointing, but it also means you can shop on your own schedule and choose the product you want. Financing can spread the cost.
