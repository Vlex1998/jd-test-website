If we had to name the single most common leak location on Houston homes with a fireplace, it would be the chimney. Not the shingles around it. The flashing. A chimney is a big rectangular hole in the roof with a masonry or framed structure poking through it, and the metal that seals the joint has to handle expansion, movement, debris, and decades of sun. When it fails, water runs down the chimney and shows up on the ceiling or the drywall next to the fireplace.

How Chimney Flashing Is Supposed to Work

A properly flashed chimney has two layers of metal working together:

  • Step flashing (base flashing). L-shaped pieces of metal woven in with each course of shingles along the sides of the chimney, with one leg under the shingle and one leg up the chimney wall.
  • Counter flashing (cap flashing). Metal set into a cut in the mortar joint (a reglet) and bent down over the top of the step flashing. It sheds water over the step flashing without being nailed to the roof, so the chimney and roof can move independently.
  • Apron flashing on the downhill face, and a cricket (a small peaked diverter) behind the uphill face on wider chimneys so water and debris do not pile up against the back.

On framed chimneys with siding or stucco, the counter flashing goes behind the siding instead of into mortar, which is where a lot of builder shortcuts happen.

Why It Fails

  • Caulk instead of counter flashing. The most common shortcut. The step flashing is just caulked to the brick. Caulk lasts a few years in Texas sun and then the joint opens.
  • Surface-mounted counter flashing. Metal screwed to the brick face and caulked, rather than let into the mortar joint. Same problem, slower.
  • No cricket. Leaves and pine needles pile up behind the chimney, hold water, and eventually rot the decking and rust the apron.
  • Rusted galvanized flashing. Original flashing on a 25-year-old roof is often rusted through.
  • Mortar joints and crown. Not flashing, but cracked mortar, a cracked chimney crown, or a missing cap lets water into the chimney itself, which then shows up as a "roof leak."
  • Reused flashing on a re-roof. A roofer who leaves the old flashing in place and shingles around it. Very common on cheap replacements.

Signs of a Chimney Leak

  • Ceiling stains near the fireplace, or on the wall above the mantel
  • Damp or efflorescence (white mineral stains) on the brick inside
  • Water in the firebox after rain
  • Rust streaks on the roof below the chimney
  • Peeling paint or wallpaper near the chimney chase
  • From the roof: lifted or gapped counter flashing, caulk that has pulled away, rust, or a debris pile behind the chimney

How It Should Be Repaired

A real repair means replacing the flashing, not sealing it. The shingles along the chimney are lifted or removed, new step flashing is woven in with each course, new counter flashing is cut into the mortar joints and sealed, a cricket is added if there was none, and the shingles are put back. On a framed chimney, siding comes off to get the counter flashing behind it. This is a few hours to a day of work and is not a caulk-gun job. See roof repair cost in Houston for pricing ranges.

If the chimney crown or mortar is the problem, that is masonry work rather than roofing, and your roofer should tell you so rather than sealing something that will not hold.

When You Replace the Roof

Every roof replacement should include new chimney flashing. Ask for it to be listed on the estimate specifically. If it is not, ask why. See how to read a roofing estimate. If the chimney has had chronic problems, a cricket should be added at the same time.

Is It Covered by Insurance?

Deteriorated flashing is wear and tear, which insurance does not cover. Flashing damaged in a specific storm (a limb strike, or wind peeling it back) can be. See does insurance cover roof leaks.
