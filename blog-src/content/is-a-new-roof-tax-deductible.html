For a roof on the home you live in, the honest answer is that it is generally not deductible on your federal return in the year you pay for it. There are exceptions, and the rules are different for rental properties, home offices, and storm losses. This article explains the general framework so you can have an informed conversation with your tax preparer. It is not tax advice, and the rules change; confirm anything here with a CPA before relying on it.

Your Primary Residence: Not Deductible, but It Raises Your Basis

The IRS treats a roof replacement on a personal residence as a capital improvement, not a repair or an expense. You cannot deduct it, but you add the cost to your home's cost basis. When you sell, a higher basis means a smaller taxable gain. For most Houston homeowners the primary residence exclusion ($250,000 single, $500,000 married) wipes out the gain anyway, but if you have owned the home a long time or it has appreciated a lot, the roof cost can matter. Keep the invoice with your permanent home records.

Rental Property: Depreciated Over Time

If the roof is on a rental, it is still a capital improvement, but you recover the cost through depreciation. A residential rental roof is depreciated over 27.5 years. Some smaller roof repairs can be expensed in the year paid under the repair regulations or safe harbors, and your CPA may be able to use other elections depending on the cost and your situation. This is one of the areas where professional advice pays for itself.

Home Office

If you claim a home office deduction using the actual expense method, a portion of the roof cost corresponding to the office's share of the home may be depreciable. Under the simplified method, it is not. Most people with a home office use the simplified method.

Storm Damage and Casualty Losses

Under current federal rules, personal casualty losses are generally deductible only when they result from a federally declared disaster. Houston has had several of those, including Harvey, the 2021 freeze, the 2024 derecho, and Hurricane Beryl. If your roof was damaged in a declared disaster, the uninsured portion of the loss (after insurance payments and subject to IRS thresholds) may be deductible. The calculation is involved and depends on your insurance payout, so this is squarely CPA territory. Keep your claim paperwork. See our Texas roof insurance claim guide.

Energy Credits

Federal residential energy credits have changed repeatedly. Roofing products such as reflective metal or asphalt shingles were removed from the eligible list several years ago, and the broader home energy credits were scheduled to end for property placed in service after 2025. Do not count on a federal credit for a roof. If a contractor tells you a roof qualifies for a tax credit, ask them for the specific code section and verify it with your CPA. Energy-efficient roofing still saves money on cooling bills; see energy-efficient roofing for Houston heat.

Solar Panels Are Different

Solar installations have historically qualified for a federal credit, and the roof work directly required to support the panels has sometimes been included. That credit has also been subject to recent legislative changes, so check the current status. If you are planning solar, read solar panels and roof replacement first.

Texas Has No State Income Tax

There is no Texas income tax deduction to think about. Property taxes are a separate question: a new roof generally does not trigger a reassessment by itself in Harris or Fort Bend County, since appraisal districts value homes on market comparables, not individual improvements. A new roof is not going to raise your property tax bill in a noticeable way.

Insurance Payouts Are Not Income

If your insurer pays for a roof, the payment is not taxable income. It is reimbursement for a loss. Depreciation recovered under an RCV policy is also not income.

Bottom Line

  • Primary residence: not deductible, adds to basis, keep the receipt.
  • Rental: depreciate, with possible expensing options for smaller work.
  • Declared disaster: uninsured loss may be deductible, ask a CPA.
  • Energy credits: do not assume one applies.
