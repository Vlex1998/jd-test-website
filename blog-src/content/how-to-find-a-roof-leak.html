The water stain on your ceiling is almost never directly under the hole in your roof. Water gets in at a shingle, a boot, or a flashing joint, runs down the underlayment or a rafter, and drops onto the drywall wherever it hits a nail or a joint, sometimes ten or fifteen feet from the source. Finding the real entry point is the whole job. Here is how to do it, and when to stop and call someone.

Start Inside

  • Note where and when the leak shows up. Only in wind-driven rain from the south? Only after long steady rain? Only when the AC runs? Each points to a different cause.
  • Check the obvious non-roof suspects. A stain near a bathroom or the AC closet may be a plumbing leak or a clogged condensate drain, not the roof. Leaks that appear in dry weather are almost never the roof.
  • Measure. Measure from two walls to the stain so you can find the same spot in the attic.

Go Into the Attic

Bring a bright flashlight and go during or right after rain if you can. Step only on joists.

  • Find the spot above the stain, then look uphill along the rafters and the underside of the decking for water trails, dark stains, or wet insulation.
  • Follow the trail up until it stops. That is close to the entry point.
  • Look for daylight around chimneys, pipes, and vents.
  • Check for wet insulation around any penetration. Shiny nails with rust drips can mean condensation rather than a leak, which is a ventilation problem. See roof ventilation and attic heat.
  • Mark the spot on the decking and measure its position so it can be found from the roof.

Then Look at the Roof

From the ground with binoculars, or from a ladder at the eave, look uphill from the spot you found. In Houston, the most common leak sources, roughly in order:

  • Pipe boots with cracked rubber. See pipe boot leaks.
  • Chimney flashing and the area behind the chimney where debris collects. See chimney flashing leaks.
  • Wall-to-roof intersections where step flashing meets siding or brick.
  • Vents, exhaust caps, and satellite mounts with failed sealant or missing screws.
  • Valleys, especially with debris or where two different slopes meet.
  • Skylights. See why skylights leak.
  • Nail pops and exposed nails that have backed out and lifted a shingle.
  • Missing, cracked, or creased shingles from wind. See wind damage signs.

The Hose Test

If you cannot find it visually and have a helper, a hose test works. One person in the attic with a flashlight, one on the roof with a garden hose. Start low, soaking one small area at a time for several minutes, working uphill. Do not spray up under the shingles; let the water run down the roof the way rain does. When the attic person sees water, you have isolated the area. This takes patience. Rushing it, or soaking the whole roof at once, tells you nothing.

Flat and Low-Slope Roofs

On flat sections, water can travel a long way under the membrane before it finds a way through. Look for ponding areas, open seams, blisters, cracked sealant at the edges, and anything that was screwed into the roof later, like a satellite dish or an AC line. These leaks are harder to trace and usually need a pro.

When to Stop and Call a Roofer

  • The roof is steep, wet, or two stories or more
  • You have traced it to a chimney or wall flashing, which needs real flashing work, not caulk
  • You cannot find a trail in the attic
  • The roof is old enough that walking on it will break shingles
  • The leak is active and getting worse. See emergency roof tarping.

Do Not Just Caulk It

Caulk over a flashing joint or a cracked boot is a six-month fix at best. The sun breaks it down, the joint moves, and the leak returns, now with more rotted decking underneath. Fix the component.
