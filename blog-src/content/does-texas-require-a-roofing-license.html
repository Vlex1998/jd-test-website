Texas does not license roofing contractors at the state level. Anyone with a truck and a ladder can legally call themselves a roofer here. That surprises a lot of homeowners, especially people who moved from states where roofing is a licensed trade. It also explains why Houston gets flooded with questionable operators after every storm. Here is what the lack of a license means and how to protect yourself.

What Texas Does Not Require

  • No state roofing contractor license
  • No required exam, training, or experience
  • No state bonding requirement
  • No required insurance, including workers' compensation

Bills to create a mandatory roofing license have been filed in the Texas Legislature several times and have not passed. For now, the state's consumer protections are mostly after the fact, through the Deceptive Trade Practices Act and the Attorney General's office.

What Texas Does Regulate

  • Insurance claim conduct. Roofers cannot act as public adjusters, cannot waive or pay your deductible, and cannot advertise that they will. See can a roofer pay your deductible.
  • Contracts for insurance-funded work must include specific language about the homeowner's deductible obligation.
  • Local registration. Many cities (Pearland, Sugar Land, League City, Pasadena, Baytown, and others) require roofers to register before pulling permits. It is not a competency test, but it creates a paper trail.
  • Windstorm construction in coastal counties must be inspected and certified. See our coastal roofing guide.

The Voluntary RCAT License

The Roofing Contractors Association of Texas runs a voluntary licensing program. To get an RCAT license, a contractor has to pass an exam, show proof of insurance, provide references, and agree to a code of conduct. It is the closest thing Texas has to a real credential, and it is a meaningful signal. The absence of one does not mean a roofer is bad; many excellent companies have not bothered. But a roofer who has one has at least demonstrated basic knowledge and carried insurance at the time of licensing.

Manufacturer Certifications

Shingle manufacturers run their own contractor programs, such as Owens Corning Preferred Contractor and CertainTeed's credential tiers, and GAF's certified and Master Elite programs. These require proof of insurance, a track record, and training on the manufacturer's products, and they allow the contractor to offer the manufacturer's enhanced warranties. They are a useful filter. See roof warranties explained.

How to Vet a Roofer in a State With No License

  • Insurance certificate for general liability and workers' comp or occupational accident coverage, sent from the agent.
  • Physical office you can visit, and a business that has existed under the same name for years. Search the Texas Secretary of State and Comptroller records.
  • Local references and jobs you can drive past.
  • BBB profile and online reviews read with attention to how the company responds to complaints.
  • Written contract naming specific products, scope, price, payment schedule, and warranty.
  • Registration with your city if one is required.

Why It Matters More After Storms

Because there is no license to pull, there is no barrier to entry after a hail storm. Crews from other states set up in a hotel, knock doors, collect insurance checks, and leave. When the roof fails two years later, the company no longer exists. Read local roofer vs storm chaser before you sign anything after a storm.

If Something Goes Wrong

With no licensing board to complain to, your options are the Texas Attorney General consumer complaint process, the BBB, small claims or civil court, and if an insurance claim was involved, the Texas Department of Insurance. A written contract and a documented paper trail make all of these far more effective.
