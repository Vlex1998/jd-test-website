When a storm opens up your roof and water is coming into the house, the first job is not fixing the roof. It is stopping the water. A properly installed tarp does that, protects your insurance claim, and buys the time you need to get a real repair scheduled. Here is when you need one, how it should be done, and what to avoid.

When You Need an Emergency Tarp

  • Shingles blew off and the underlayment or decking is exposed
  • A tree or limb punctured the roof
  • Hail cracked a skylight or vent and rain is coming through
  • Water is actively dripping through a ceiling and more rain is forecast
  • A flat or low-slope roof seam has opened up

If it is a slow drip from a small leak and the weather is clear, you probably do not need a tarp. You need a repair. Tarps are for open roofs and imminent rain.

Your Insurance Expects It

Every homeowners policy requires you to take reasonable steps to prevent further damage after a loss. Tarping is the textbook example. If you do nothing and the next storm ruins the ceilings and floors, the insurer can deny that additional damage. The cost of emergency tarping is normally reimbursable under the claim, so keep the invoice and photos. See our Texas roof insurance claim guide.

First Steps Inside the House

  • Move furniture and electronics out from under the leak.
  • Put a bucket under the drip. If a ceiling is bulging with water, poke a small hole in the lowest point with a screwdriver to drain it into the bucket before it collapses.
  • Photograph everything before moving or cleaning up. See what to do right after a storm.
  • Turn off power to a room if water is near fixtures or outlets.

How a Proper Tarp Is Installed

A tarp thrown over the damage and weighed down with bricks will blow off in the next gust. A proper emergency tarp:

  • Extends past the damage at least three to four feet on every side and runs up and over the ridge if the damage is near the top, so water cannot get in from above.
  • Is anchored with wood strips (furring strips or 2x4s) rolled into the edges and screwed through the tarp into the roof deck. This spreads the load and keeps the wind from tearing the grommets out.
  • Is pulled tight so water sheds instead of pooling.
  • Uses a heavy-duty tarp, not a thin blue one from the hardware store. Those shred in a few days of sun and wind.

Yes, this means screw holes in your roof. On a roof that is about to be repaired or replaced, that is irrelevant. The holes are patched with the repair.

Should You Do It Yourself?

Climbing a wet, damaged roof during or right after a storm is how homeowners get seriously hurt. If the roof is steep, wet, or more than a single story, call a roofer. Most Houston roofing companies, including ours, offer emergency tarping and can get there the same day after a storm. If you do go up, wait for the rain to stop, use a stable ladder, wear soft-soled shoes, and have someone on the ground.

Beware the Free Tarp

After big storms, crews go door to door offering "free" tarps. The catch is usually a contract that locks you into using that company for the full repair, sometimes with an assignment of your insurance benefits. Read anything before you sign it. A legitimate emergency tarp is a simple service with a simple invoice. See local roofer vs storm chaser.

How Long Can a Tarp Stay On?

A well-installed heavy tarp lasts a few months. It is not a roof. UV breaks it down, wind works the edges loose, and water eventually finds a way under. Use the time to get your claim moving and the repair scheduled. After large storms, roofers in Houston book out weeks, so start the process immediately rather than waiting for the tarp to fail.
