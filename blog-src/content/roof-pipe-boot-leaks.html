Every house has plumbing vent pipes that stick up through the roof, and every one of those pipes has a boot around it: a metal or plastic base with a rubber collar that seals against the pipe. The rubber lasts about 8 to 12 years in Houston sun. The shingles around it last 20 or more. That mismatch makes the pipe boot the most common source of roof leaks we fix, and one of the cheapest.

How a Pipe Boot Fails

The rubber collar is exposed to UV all day, every day. It dries out, hardens, and cracks, usually in a ring around the pipe where the rubber is stretched tightest. Once it splits, water runs straight down the outside of the pipe into the attic. Because the pipe goes through the ceiling of a bathroom or kitchen, the stain shows up there, and homeowners often assume it is a plumbing problem first.

Other failure modes:

  • Collar slid down the pipe after the rubber lost its grip, leaving a gap at the top.
  • Rusted metal base on older galvanized boots.
  • Cracked plastic base from heat and foot traffic.
  • Exposed nails on the base flange that were never sealed or where sealant failed.
  • Squirrels and rats chewing the rubber, which is more common in Houston than you would think.

How to Tell

  • Ceiling stain in a bathroom, kitchen, or laundry room, or in a hallway near one
  • A leak that shows up only in rain, never in dry weather (a plumbing leak does not care about weather)
  • In the attic, water trails on the outside of a vent pipe or wet insulation around its base
  • From the ground with binoculars, a visible crack or a collar that is lifted or sunken

Our guide to finding a roof leak covers the attic check.

The Fix

Replacing a pipe boot means lifting the shingles around it, removing the old boot, sliding on a new one with the upper flange under the shingles and the lower flange over them, sealing the nails, and laying the shingles back. It takes an experienced roofer about 20 to 30 minutes per boot. Cost is typically a few hundred dollars including the trip, and less per boot when several are done at once. Shortcuts to avoid:

  • Caulking the crack. It holds for a season and then the crack opens wider.
  • Slip-on retrofit collars that sit on top of the old boot. They work in some cases but trap water in others, and the old boot is still failing underneath.
  • Tape. No.

Better Boots

If you are replacing the roof or replacing boots on a roof with life left, ask for upgraded boots. Options include silicone collars, which outlast standard EPDM rubber, and lead or all-metal boots with no rubber at all. Lead boots have been used for a century and effectively last the life of the roof, though squirrels occasionally chew them. The upcharge is small compared to another leak in ten years.

Do Them All at Once

If one boot has failed, the rest are the same age and the same material. When we replace one, we check the others. If they are cracking, replacing all of them in the same visit saves a return trip and the next ceiling stain.

At Roof Replacement

New boots on every pipe should be standard on a replacement, and the estimate should list them. A roofer who reuses old boots on a new roof is building in a leak. See how to read a roofing estimate. Other roof penetrations, such as exhaust vents, furnace flues, and attic fans, have their own flashing that should be checked in the same pass.
