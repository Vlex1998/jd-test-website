Technically, yes, in some cases. Building code allows one layer of new asphalt shingles over one existing layer if the old roof is in reasonable shape. In practice, a roof-over (also called an overlay or a second layer) is almost never the right choice in Houston, and most reputable roofers will not do one. Here is what the code says, what the trade-offs are, and why we recommend a tear-off nearly every time.

What the Code Allows

The International Residential Code, which Houston-area cities adopt with local amendments, permits a new roof covering over an existing one with limits. A new layer is not allowed when:

  • There are already two or more layers of any roof covering
  • The existing roof is water-soaked or deteriorated to the point that it is not an adequate base
  • The existing roof is wood shake, slate, clay, cement, or asbestos-cement tile
  • The existing roof is in a windstorm zone with specific deck requirements

So a single layer of flat, sound asphalt shingles can legally be roofed over. Whether it should be is a different question.

Why People Consider It

Cost. Skipping the tear-off saves the labor of removal, the dumpster, and the disposal fee. That is usually a 15 to 25 percent savings on the job. It is also faster and less messy.

Why It Is Usually a Mistake

  • You cannot inspect the decking. The whole point of tear-off is to see the wood. Rot, delamination, and bad fasteners stay hidden under two layers and keep getting worse.
  • Heat. Two layers of asphalt hold more heat. In a Houston attic that is already 140 degrees, that cooks the new shingles from below and shortens their life.
  • Weight. A second layer adds several hundred pounds per square. On older or marginal framing it causes sag. See why roofs sag.
  • Telegraphing. Every curl, bump, and lifted tab in the old roof shows through the new one. The roof looks wavy from day one.
  • Warranty. Most manufacturers reduce or void their warranty on a roof-over, and the enhanced warranties through certified contractors are not available at all. See roof warranties explained.
  • Flashing. Proper flashing at chimneys, walls, and valleys requires getting under the shingles. On an overlay, flashing is usually left in place and caulked, which is where the leaks will start. See chimney flashing leaks.
  • Wind. Nails have to go through two layers of shingles to reach the deck. Nail hold is worse and the roof is more prone to blow-offs.
  • Insurance. Some Texas carriers rate or decline homes with layered roofs, and a future claim may only pay to remove one layer.
  • The next roof costs more. When the overlay eventually fails, the next crew has to tear off two layers. You paid to defer the cost, not avoid it.

When It Might Make Sense

Honestly, rarely. The only cases where we see a reasonable argument:

  • A small, simple, low-value outbuilding or shed where cost matters more than longevity
  • A home that will be demolished or heavily renovated within a few years

Even then, the savings are modest and the downside is real.

What About Metal Over Shingles?

Metal panels over a single layer of shingles are a more defensible overlay, because metal is light, is screwed through to the framing or to purlins, and does not telegraph the old roof the same way. It is commonly done on barns and some houses. Decking still cannot be inspected, and a layer of underlayment or slip sheet is required between the shingles and metal. See metal roofing in Texas.

If a Roofer Suggests a Roof-Over

Ask why. If the answer is to save you money, ask for a tear-off price for comparison and compare the warranties. If the answer is that the decking is fine so there is no need to look at it, be skeptical, because no one knows the decking is fine until it is exposed. See questions to ask a roofer before hiring.
