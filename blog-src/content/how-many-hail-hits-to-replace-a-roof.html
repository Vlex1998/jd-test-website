When an insurance adjuster inspects a roof for hail, they are not looking at the whole roof at once. They mark out a test square, count the hits inside it, and apply a threshold. Understanding how that count works explains why one adjuster approves a full replacement and another offers to pay for a few shingles.

The Test Square

A test square is a 10-foot by 10-foot area, which is one roofing square or 100 square feet. The adjuster chalks one out on each slope, usually on the slope that faced the storm, and counts every hail impact inside it. Hits are marked with chalk circles so they can be photographed.

The Threshold

Most carriers in Texas consider a slope damaged beyond repair when a test square shows roughly 8 to 10 hail hits. Some use 6, some use 10 or more, and a few look at the whole slope rather than a square. If enough slopes exceed the threshold, the roof is replaced. If only one slope qualifies, some carriers will pay for that slope only, and others will replace the roof because the new shingles will not match the old.

This is not law. It is an industry convention that varies by company and sometimes by adjuster. Ask what threshold your adjuster is using.

What Counts as a Hit

Not every mark on a shingle is hail. A legitimate hail hit on an asphalt shingle has:

  • A bruise or soft spot where the mat under the granules has been fractured. Press it with a thumb and it gives.
  • Granule loss in a roughly circular pattern exposing the black asphalt underneath.
  • Random distribution across the slope, since hail does not fall in a pattern.
  • Matching impacts on soft metals: dents in vents, gutters, downspouts, AC fins, and window screens. These are the best proof that hail of a given size fell on the house.

Things that get mistaken for hail: blister pops from heat, mechanical damage from foot traffic, nail pops, and granule loss from age. Experienced adjusters can tell the difference, which is why a roofer who knows what they are doing should be at the inspection. See how to spot hail damage.

Why Adjusters Disagree

  • Which slope got the test square. The storm-facing slope typically has three times the hits of the back slope.
  • Lighting and time of day. Hail bruises show best in low-angle morning or late afternoon light. A midday inspection in flat light misses hits.
  • Experience. After a large storm, carriers bring in temporary adjusters from out of state, some with very little roofing background.
  • Shingle age. On an old roof with heavy granule loss, it is genuinely hard to separate hail from wear, and the adjuster will lean toward wear.

What If the Count Is Below the Threshold?

If your roof has real hail damage but not enough to trigger replacement, the carrier may pay for a repair, or nothing if the repair cost is below your deductible. You can request a re-inspection with your roofer present. If the count is close, a second look in better light often changes the result. See what to do if your roof claim is denied.

Hail Damage That Is Not Visible

Hail hits fracture the fiberglass mat under the granules. Even when the surface looks fine, that fracture lets water in slowly and the shingle fails in a year or two. Carriers generally only pay for what can be seen and documented, which is why a prompt inspection after a storm matters. Waiting a year means the bruises weather in and become hard to prove.

Protecting Yourself for Next Time

If you are replacing the roof after hail, consider Class 4 impact-resistant shingles. They take considerably more punishment before bruising, which means fewer claims, and most Texas insurers discount the premium for them.
